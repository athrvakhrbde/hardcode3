India's Digital Surveillance Architecture: A Map for the Uninitiated


India has quietly built one of the most extensive digital surveillance architectures in any democracy. No single law authorises it. No single agency controls it. It runs on executive fiat, post-26/11 urgency, and a 1.4-billion-person identity database that was supposed to be "just verification."

The Architecture

India's surveillance stack is a layered system. Each tool covers a different attack surface, and together they form a 360-degree profile.

System What it does Legal basis
NATGRID Links 20+ databases for real-time intelligence queries — travel, banking, telecom, Aadhaar, social media Executive order, no statute
Central Monitoring System (CMS) Real-time interception of calls, texts, and internet traffic Section 69, IT Act
Facial Recognition & Ikshana Live crowd scanning via FRT vans at protests and public spaces None confirmed
Aadhaar & Identity Infrastructure 1.1 billion+ biometric records, increasingly linked to other systems Aadhaar Act (limited scope)
Internet Shutdowns Cutting connectivity to control information flow during protests Telegraph Act, Telecom Suspension Rules
Pegasus & Spyware NSO Group's Pegasus used against Indian journalists, activists, politicians Supreme Court investigation (sealed report)

CMS listens to what a suspect says, while NATGRID tracks where they go and what they own — together forming a 360-degree profile.

The Legal Framework

India's surveillance regime rests on a patchwork of laws, none of which alone authorises the full stack.

  • Puttaswamy judgment (2017) — Declared privacy a fundamental right and set a three-part test: legality, legitimate aim, proportionality.
  • DPDP Act 2023 — India's data protection law, with sweeping government exemptions for "security" and "public order".
  • Section 69, IT Act — The government's catch-all interception power.
  • Section 5(2), Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 — The colonial-era interception law.

The gap: India has a right-to-privacy judgment and a data protection law, but no comprehensive surveillance reform legislation. No judicial oversight for interception. No statutory basis for NATGRID, FRT, or many other systems.

The three-part test, applied to the architecture:

Test Fails for
Legality NATGRID (no statute), FRT (no enabling law), CMS interception (Section 69 too broad)
Legitimate aim NATGRID's expansion into routine financial/civil cases, FRT matching against "habitual offender" databases
Proportionality Mass facial recognition (scanning every face to find a few), NATGRID's 119-crore-person NPR integration, CMS interception without individualised suspicion

Key Takeaways

  • No single law authorises the whole system — it's a stack of executive orders, colonial-era laws, and regulatory gaps.
  • The three-part test is consistently failed — legality, legitimate aim, and proportionality all break down across the architecture.
  • Aadhaar is the connective tissue — not a surveillance tool itself, but the identity infrastructure that makes every other surveillance tool more powerful.
  • Shutdowns are surveillance-adjacent — they don't just block communication; they push people onto channels that are easier to tap (SMS, calls → CMS).
  • The resistance exists — IFF's litigation, Garudex's documentation, Project Panoptic's tracking — but the legal and institutional gaps remain vast.
